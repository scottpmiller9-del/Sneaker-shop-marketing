The sneaker shop marketing plan

How to get more customers into an independent sneaker shop, step by step. This is the same plan I run for my paying clients, written out so you can see exactly what the work involves. Use it yourself, or hire me to run it every month.

By Scott Miller · Updated November 15, 2026 · About 15 minutes

Here's the honest version of most shop owners' week. Buying pairs at the counter, checking authenticity, pricing, shooting, listing, answering DMs, closing up. Marketing happens at 11 p.m. if it happens at all. A boosted post here. An email when you remember.

That isn't a discipline problem. It's a time problem. And guessing costs real money, because every dollar you test on ads that don't work is a dollar that didn't go into inventory.

So here's the plan, in the order I'd do it, with the numbers behind each step. Order matters. Most shops start with ads, which is backwards. Ads pour traffic onto whatever you already have. If the foundation leaks, you're paying to fill a bucket with a hole in it.

70%of online carts get abandoned before checkout1
97%of consumers read reviews for local businesses2
2%of email sends, the automated ones, drove 30% of email revenue3
5–25×more to win a new customer than to keep one4

The plan

1. Get your numbers straight

Before any of the fun stuff, you need three numbers every month: new customers, revenue, and gross profit. Not followers. Not clicks.

Shopify already reports new versus returning customers, sales by channel, and gross profit if your product costs are filled in. That last part is where shops fall down. If cost per item is blank, your profit numbers are fiction. Fill it in at intake. Every pair.

Then split customers into new and returning and watch both. Here's why the returning number deserves your attention: Bain's research found a 5% bump in customer retention can lift profits anywhere from 25% to 95%4. A customer who already trusts you is the cheapest sale you'll ever make.

From my own client work: at one independent shop I run marketing for, repeat customers on the online store were up 39% from March to October 2026 compared with the same months in 2025. That shop gets a new-arrivals email every Friday without fail. More on email in step 6.

Last setup piece: connect Google Analytics and purchase tracking so Google Ads knows which clicks became orders. Shopify's Google & YouTube app handles most of it. Without it, Google is optimizing your ad money blind.

2. Fix your product feed

This is the step nobody talks about, and it's broken at almost every shop I look at.

Your Shopify catalog feeds Google Merchant Center and Meta. That feed powers Shopping ads and the product listings people see on Google. When the data is wrong, products get disapproved or shown to the wrong people. Quietly. No alert. Your Shopping ads just shrink.

How common is it? In a DataFeedWatch study of 15,000 stores, 7% of feed items were rejected for data errors, and 16.66% of catalog products were out of stock5. That's 2022 data from a feed-tool vendor, so treat it as a baseline. But in a sneaker shop, with one-of-one pairs selling daily, I'd bet on worse.

The usual problems:

  • Condition is wrong or blank. Google accepts new, used, or refurbished. A pre-owned pair sent as new is a policy problem and a customer service problem.
  • Bad barcodes. Resale pairs often carry internal SKUs in the barcode field. Google reads those as product IDs and rejects them. If a pair doesn't have a real one, mark it as having no identifier.
  • Missing gender, age group, and size. Footwear needs them. Without them, products get limited or dropped.
  • Messy brand names. Jordan, JORDAN, and Air Jordan as three different brands. Pick one.
  • Sold-out pairs still live. Every click on a pair you don't have is wasted money.

Fix it in Shopify, not Merchant Center. Google and Meta both pull from your catalog, so one fix covers both.

Nugget

If you have a physical store, Google can show in-store stock too, through local inventory ads and free local product listings5. Someone searching a specific Jordan 4 nearby can see that your shop has it on the wall today. If you have a counter, turn it on.

3. Build product pages that rank and sell

Of every ten carts started online, about seven never check out. Baymard's average across 50 studies is 70.22%1.

The top reason shoppers give is surprise costs at checkout: shipping, taxes, fees6. So say your shipping price and return policy right on the product page, before anyone hits checkout.

Then answer the four questions a sneaker buyer has before anything else. Condition, year, box, what's included. Top of every description, same order, every time:

Condition: Pre-owned, 9/10. Light creasing on the toe box.
Year: 2023
Box: Original box, lid has minor wear
Includes: Extra laces

Below it, a few real sentences. What makes the colorway worth owning. How it fits. Write it like you'd talk about it at the counter.

For titles, use what people type: brand, model, colorway, and the style code from the box label. Someone searching a style code knows exactly what they want. That's the buyer you want landing on your page instead of a marketplace. Do the same for collection pages. A Jordan 4 collection with a short, useful intro pulls searches a bare grid never will.

4. Own your local search

Reviews aren't a nice-to-have anymore. They're the gate. BrightLocal's 2026 survey of US consumers found:

97%read reviews for local businesses2
47%won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews7
74%only care about reviews from the last three months7
89%expect the owner to reply to reviews7

Read those together. A shop with 300 great reviews from 2023 and nothing since looks closed to a lot of people. You need a steady drip of new ones. A small sign at the register and a line in your order confirmation email do the job. Reply to every review, in your own words. Half of consumers are put off by replies that sound templated7.

That jump in AI tools is the nugget here. When someone asks ChatGPT for a sneaker shop near them, it pulls from what's written about you on Google, your website, and review sites. Clear, consistent info everywhere is how you show up in that answer.

Your Google Business Profile checklist:

  • Hours that are actually correct, holidays included
  • Fresh photos of the wall, the counter, and new arrivals, added regularly
  • A description that says you buy, sell, and trade, if you do
  • A website link that points to your store, with Instagram in the social links

More than one location? Give each store its own page on your site with its address, hours, phone, and a few lines about that store, plus its own Business Profile. One "Locations" page with three addresses doesn't help any of them rank.

5. Run Google Ads on what's actually in stock

Now the feed is clean and the pages are solid. Ads finally have something worth sending people to.

Shopping ads first. They show the shoe, the price, and your shop name right in the results. For a product people buy by sight, that beats a text ad. They run off your feed, so they only show pairs you have, as long as you did step two.

Search ads for your name and your area. Cover your shop name so a competitor can't sit on top of it. Then target people looking for a sneaker store in your city.

Search ads for sellers. If you buy over the counter, run a small campaign for people trying to sell sneakers near you. Inventory bought at the right price is margin. Few shops advertise this side at all.

Nugget

Put your budget behind what's moving. StockX's 2025 data: Nike, Jordan, adidas, New Balance, and ASICS were the top-selling sneaker brands for the third year running8. Running silhouettes jumped 45% year over year, and Kobe 6 Protro sales more than doubled9. If those are on your wall, they belong in your ads and your Friday email.

Start with a budget you're fine losing while it learns. Check it weekly. Cut what doesn't produce orders or buys, move the money to what does. The goal isn't clicks. It's new customers at a cost that leaves profit.

The trap: boosting Instagram posts and calling it advertising. A boost mostly reaches people who already follow you. Fine for loyalty. Not how you find new customers.

6. Set up the three emails that sell while you sleep

Here's the most lopsided stat in this whole guide. Omnisend looked at 27 billion emails from 150,000 brands in 2025. Automated emails, the ones triggered by what a shopper does, were 2% of sends but drove 30% of email revenue3.

So the weekly blast matters, but the money is in automations. Build these three first:

  1. Welcome email. Fires when someone joins your list. Show your best stuff and how buying, selling, and trading with you works.
  2. Abandoned cart. Fires when someone leaves a pair in the cart. Together, welcome and abandoned cart emails drove 76% of all automation orders in Omnisend's data10.
  3. Back in stock. Fires when a size or model someone wanted comes back. This is the sneaker shop secret weapon.

Back-in-stock emails convert better than any other automation type10. And Omnisend's benchmarks show only 0.6% of brands use them12. For a shop where people are hunting one size in one colorway, that's free money sitting on the table.

Then keep the weekly email. Pick a day and stick to it. I like Friday, right before weekend money and weekend time. Same format every week so it's fast to build: a subject line naming the best pair that came in, a line or two in your voice, about six new arrivals with prices, your latest video if you post one, and one button to shop. Shopify's built-in email tool handles all of it.

7. Add text marketing for your best buyers

Texts get read fast, which makes them right for anything time-sensitive: a drop hitting the shelf, early access to a sale, a restock of a size people kept asking about.

Same rule as email. Automate it. In Omnisend's 2025 data, automated texts earned $0.74 per send versus $0.15 for one-off campaigns13. Average click rate on text campaigns was 12.39%11.

Keep it rare and valuable. Text too often and people opt out. A good first move: a signup offer for early access to your next big sale, using a gentle prompt instead of a pop-up that blocks the whole screen.

One rule that isn't optional: get clear opt-in before you text anyone marketing messages. Your texting app handles the consent language. Don't upload a list of numbers you collected for something else.

8. Turn what you already film into search traffic

Lots of shops already post walk-throughs, pickups, and new-arrival videos. That footage is sitting on platforms people search.

BrightLocal's 2026 survey found 37% of US consumers use Instagram and 29% use TikTok to find reviews of local businesses2. Your videos are reviews now, whether you planned it or not.

Give every YouTube video a real title and description. Name the shoes, the shop, and the city. Link to the products shown. Same footage, far more people finding it.

A blog helps too, if it answers what your customers ask. How to spot a fake pair. How to clean suede. What sells for the most at a buy counter. Write it in your shop's voice and link to the products you mention. Two solid posts a month beats ten thin ones.

9. Review it every month

Once a month, twenty minutes, same three numbers from step one. New customers. Revenue. Gross profit. Compare them to last month and to the same month last year.

Then three questions. What brought in new customers? What did we spend on that didn't work? What do we do more of next month? Write the answers down. A one-page report you actually read beats a dashboard you never open.

The short version

Set up once: Fill in product costs. Fix the feed. Rewrite product page tops. Build a page per location. Turn on welcome, abandoned cart, and back-in-stock emails.

Every week: Send the Friday email. Check ad spend against orders. Keep sold-out pairs off the feed. Ask for reviews and reply to new ones.

Every month: Pull new customers, revenue, and gross profit. Cut what didn't work. Publish two useful blog posts. Clean up disapproved products.
