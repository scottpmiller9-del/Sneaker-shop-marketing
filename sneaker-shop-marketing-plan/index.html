The sneaker shop marketing plan

How to get more customers into an independent sneaker shop, step by step. This is the same plan I run for the shops I work with. Take it and do it yourself, or call me and I'll run it.

By Scott Miller · Updated November 15, 2026 · About 12 minutes

Here's the honest version of most shop owners' week. You're buying pairs at the counter, checking authenticity, pricing, shooting photos, listing, answering DMs, and closing up. Marketing happens at 11 p.m. if it happens at all. A boosted post here. An email when you remember.

That isn't a discipline problem. It's a time problem. And the cost of guessing is real, because every dollar you test on ads that don't work is a dollar that didn't go into inventory.

So here's the plan, in the order I'd do it. Order matters. Most shops start with ads, which is backwards. Ads pour traffic onto whatever you already have, so if the foundation leaks, you're paying to fill a bucket with a hole in it.

The plan

  1. Get your numbers straight
  2. Fix your product feed
  3. Build product pages that rank and sell
  4. Own your local search
  5. Run Google Ads on what's actually in stock
  6. Email your customers every week
  7. Add text marketing for your best buyers
  8. Turn what you already film into search traffic
  9. Review it every month

1. Get your numbers straight

Before any of the fun stuff, you need to know three things every month: how many new customers you got, how much revenue came in, and how much of it you kept as gross profit. Not followers. Not clicks. Customers, sales, profit.

If you're on Shopify, most of this is already sitting in your analytics. Shopify reports new versus returning customers, sales by channel, and gross profit if your product costs are filled in. That last part is where a lot of shops fall down. If cost per item is blank, your profit numbers are fiction. Fill it in at intake, every pair, every time.

Then connect Google Analytics and set up purchase tracking so Google Ads knows which clicks turned into orders. Shopify's Google & YouTube app handles most of the connection. Without it, Google is optimizing your ads blind.

Do this week: Pull last month's new customers, revenue, and gross profit. Write the three numbers down. That's your baseline. Everything after this gets measured against it.

2. Fix your product feed

This is the step nobody talks about, and it's the one I find broken at almost every shop I look at.

Your Shopify catalog feeds Google Merchant Center and Meta. That feed is what powers Shopping ads and the product listings people see on Google. If the data in it is wrong, Google disapproves products or shows them to the wrong people. Quietly. You don't get an alert. Your Shopping ads just get smaller.

The usual problems in a sneaker shop's feed:

  • Condition is wrong or blank. Google accepts new, used, or refurbished. A pre-owned pair sent as new is a policy problem and a customer service problem.
  • Bad barcodes. Resale pairs often carry internal SKUs in the barcode field. Google reads those as product identifiers and rejects them. If a pair doesn't have a real one, mark it as having no identifier instead of guessing.
  • Missing gender, age group, and size. Apparel and footwear need these. Without them, products get limited or dropped.
  • Messy brand names. Jordan, JORDAN, and Air Jordan as three different brands. Pick one and clean it up.
  • Sold-out pairs still live. Every click on a pair you don't have is wasted money and an annoyed shopper.

Fix it in Shopify, not in Merchant Center. Both Google and Meta pull from your catalog, so one fix in the source covers both channels.

3. Build product pages that rank and sell

A sneaker buyer wants four answers before anything else. What condition is it in. What year. Is the box there. What comes with it. Put those at the very top of every product description, in the same order, every time:

Condition: Pre-owned, 9/10. Light creasing on the toe box.
Year: 2023
Box: Original box, lid has minor wear
Includes: Extra laces

Then write a few real sentences below it. What makes this colorway worth owning. How it fits. What people pair it with. Write it like you'd talk about it at the counter, because that's the voice your customers trust.

For titles, use the words people actually type: brand, model, colorway, and the style code from the box label. Someone searching a specific style code knows exactly what they want. That's the buyer you want landing on your page instead of a marketplace.

Do the same thing for collection pages. A Jordan 4 collection page with a short, useful intro will pull searches that a bare grid of products never will.

4. Own your local search

For a shop with a counter, a big chunk of new customers start with a search like "sneaker store near me" or "sell my Jordans near me." That second one matters more than most owners realize. Buying inventory is half your business, and people looking to sell are searching too.

Start with your Google Business Profile. It's free and it's often the first thing a new customer sees.

  • Hours that are actually correct, including holidays
  • Fresh photos of the wall, the counter, and new arrivals, added regularly
  • A description that says you buy, sell, and trade, if you do
  • Replies to every review, good and bad
  • A website link that points to your store, with Instagram in the social links

Then ask for reviews. Every happy customer at the register is a review you're leaving on the table. A small sign by the counter or a line in your order confirmation email does the job.

If you have more than one location, give each store its own page on your website with its address, hours, phone number, and a few lines about that store. Each location should also have its own Business Profile. A single "Locations" page with three addresses on it doesn't help any of them rank.

5. Run Google Ads on what's actually in stock

Now that the feed is clean and the pages are solid, ads have something worth sending people to.

Shopping ads first. They show the shoe, the price, and your shop name right in the search results. For a product people buy by sight, that beats a text ad. Because they run off your feed, they only show pairs you have, as long as you did step two.

Search ads for your name and your area. Cover searches for your shop name so a competitor can't sit on top of it. Then target local searches from people looking for a sneaker store in your city.

Search ads for sellers. If you buy pairs over the counter, run a small campaign for people trying to sell sneakers near you. Inventory you buy at the right price is margin. Most shops never advertise this side at all.

Start with a budget you're fine losing while it learns. Check it every week. Turn off what doesn't produce orders or sell-ins, and move that money to what does. The goal isn't clicks. It's new customers at a cost that leaves you profit.

The trap to avoid: boosting Instagram posts and calling it advertising. A boost reaches people who already follow you. That's fine for loyalty. It's not how you find new customers.

6. Email your customers every week

The cheapest customer you'll ever get is one who already bought from you. Email is how you bring them back, and most shops send it once a month at best. Or never.

Pick a day and stick to it. I like Friday, right before people have weekend money and weekend time. Keep the format the same every week so it's fast to build:

  • A subject line that names the best pair that came in
  • A short line or two in your shop's voice
  • A grid of about six new arrivals with names and prices
  • Your latest video, if you post on YouTube
  • One button to shop

That's it. You don't need a designer or a fancy flow builder to start. Shopify's built-in email tool handles all of it. The win is consistency. A customer who hears from you every Friday thinks of you before they open an app.

Grow the list at the counter, too. Ask for an email at checkout and give people a reason, like first look at new arrivals.

7. Add text marketing for your best buyers

Texts get read fast. That makes them right for things that are time-sensitive: a drop hitting the shelf, early access to a sale, a restock of a size people kept asking for.

Keep it rare and keep it valuable. Text too often and people unsubscribe. A good starting move is a signup offer for early access to your next big sale. Use a gentle signup prompt on your site instead of a pop-up that blocks the whole screen on the first second.

One rule that isn't optional: get clear opt-in before you text anyone marketing messages. Your texting app handles the consent language. Don't upload a list of numbers you collected for something else.

8. Turn what you already film into search traffic

A lot of shops already post walk-throughs, pickups, and new-arrival videos. That's great content sitting on a platform that's also a search engine.

Give every YouTube video a real title and description. Name the shoes in it, the shop, and the city. Add a link back to the products shown. Same footage, far more people finding it.

A blog helps too, if it answers what your customers actually ask. How to spot a fake pair. How to clean suede. What sells for the most at a buy counter. Write it in your shop's voice and link to the products you mention. Two solid posts a month beats ten thin ones.

9. Review it every month

Once a month, sit down for twenty minutes with the same three numbers from step one. New customers. Revenue. Gross profit. Compare them to last month and to the same month last year.

Then ask three questions. What brought in new customers? What did we spend money on that didn't? What do we do more of next month? Write the answers down. Keep it short. A one-page monthly report you actually read is worth more than a dashboard you never open.

The short version

Every week: Send the Friday email. Check ad spend against orders. Keep sold-out pairs off your feed. Post new photos to your Business Profile.

Every month: Pull new customers, revenue, and gross profit. Cut what didn't work. Publish two useful blog posts. Clean up any disapproved products.

Once, then maintain: Fix the feed. Fill in product costs. Rewrite product page tops. Build a page for each location.
